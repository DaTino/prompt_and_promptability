# Claude Ainesbrough, 12th October 1815

*Ainesbrough Hall, near Ashbourne, Derbyshire*
*Thursday, 12th October 1815*

Dear Miss Torrence,

On the morning your family left Buxton, your father was kind enough to give me leave to write to you. I thanked him with a composure I did not feel, and I have since begun this letter four times. I intend this to be the fifth and final attempt, for my man has begun to regard the good paper in the grate with a reproachful eye.

I hope your journey home into Cheshire was a tolerable one, and that you reached home before the rain. I hope too that Mrs. Torrence is quite recovered from the cold she took at the Crescent. Buxton has been very dull since you went away. The waters are no worse than they were, but the company is a great deal duller, and I have been obliged to discuss the harvest with Colonel Ashby for three evenings together.

You will be pleased to hear that I have kept my promise. I sent to London for the novel you defended so warmly in the assembly rooms, *Pride and Prejudice*, by the author of *Sense and Sensibility*, and I am now half-way through the second volume. I confess that you were right and I was wrong. It is not at all a book for silly young ladies, unless every gentleman in it is meant as a warning to me in particular. I find I have a good deal in common with Mr. Darcy, though I hope I am not so proud, and I am very certain I am not so rich. When I have finished it I shall want your opinion of Mr. Collins, whom I suspect you will defend for the pleasure of contradicting me.

There is little to report of my own doings that would interest you. We have had good sport among the partridges. My brother has bought a hunter he cannot afford. The whole neighbourhood talks of nothing but Bonaparte's voyage to St. Helena, and whether the *Northumberland* will have him there before Christmas. I confess I am more interested in a report from town that Sir Humphry Davy has been applied to by the colliery owners of the north, who want him to contrive a lamp that will keep their men safe from fire-damp. Men have been lost to it in the pits on the eastern side of this county. If a chemist can do what the gentlemen of England have not, I shall think my winter at the Royal Institution was not wasted after all. You laughed at me for that enthusiasm, but you laughed kindly, which is more than my brother has ever done.

If your father will allow it, I should be very glad to ride over and call upon your family before the frosts set in. I would ask nothing more than half an hour in your drawing-room, and your verdict on Mr. Collins.

Pray give my compliments to Mrs. Torrence, and my respects to your father. I expect he will read this before you do, and I hope I have given him no cause to regret his kindness.

I have the honour to remain, Madam,
Your most obedient humble servant,

**Claude Ainesbrough**

*P.S. I have not yet told my brother that you consider chemistry a respectable pursuit for a gentleman. I am saving it for Christmas.*
