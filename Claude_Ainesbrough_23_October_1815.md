# Claude Ainesbrough, 23rd October 1815

*Ainesbrough Hall, near Ashbourne, Derbyshire*
*Monday, 23rd October 1815*

Dear Miss Torrence,

I stand corrected on every point, and I find I do not mind it in the least. I am sorry the rain caught you so soon, and sorrier still that it confirmed Mrs. Torrence in her opinion of the Derbyshire air. In justice to my county, I must point out that the rain was very probably Cheshire rain, which had merely come across the border to meet you. I am heartily glad to hear she is recovered. I confess I was a little dismayed to learn that your home is in Hampshire. I had not understood that it lay at so great a distance, and I am the more grateful to your aunt for keeping you in the north a few weeks longer.

I have finished the novel. It cost me the better part of two nights, and my man found me asleep in my chair with the third volume open upon my knee. You shall have my opinion in full when we meet, but I will give you the substance of it now. I have concluded that the book is less about pride on one side and prejudice on the other than about two clever people who must each learn to own that they were mistaken, and who are a good deal improved by it. I hope you are right that I am readier to make such an admission than Mr. Darcy. I mean to practise it in your company until I am perfect.

As to Mr. Collins, I had hoped for an argument and you have refused me one. Your grandfather's profession has every right to disown him, and I withdraw the charge entirely. Since we must disagree upon something, I propose to defend Lady Catherine de Bourgh instead. I shall argue that a woman who takes so close an interest in her neighbours' shelves and closets is only doing what every lady in Buxton does, and with greater candour. I give you fair warning that I do not believe a word of it.

I shall not tell my brother of your opinion, or of its cause. He would not believe the second part, and I am not sure I believe it myself. I can only say that I never explained anything to anyone with more pleasure, and that I have read the reports of Sir Humphry's work with greater attention since you were good enough to take an interest in it.

Pray thank your father for his very kind invitation, which I accept with the greatest pleasure. It is not thirty miles from here to Congleton by way of Leek. If the weather holds, I shall ride over on Thursday, the 2nd of November, and put up at the Lion and Swan. I will wait upon your family on the Friday morning, if that should be convenient to Mrs. Ashcombe. A better fire than the Crescent's is no great boast, but I shall hold your mother to it.

Pray give my compliments to Mrs. Ashcombe, whose kindness to her niece I already have reason to be grateful for, and to Mrs. Torrence, and my respects to your father.

I remain, Madam,
Your most obedient and faithful servant,

**Claude Ainesbrough**

*P.S. Since we are making confessions, I will own that I wrote my postscript first, and the rest of the letter afterwards, as an excuse for sending it. I am very glad to know that yours was not written in a moment either.*
