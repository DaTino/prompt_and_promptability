At Mrs. Ashcombe’s house, near Congleton, Cheshire

Thursday, 26th October 1815

Dear Mr. Ainesbrough,

I fear the question of whether the rain belonged to Cheshire or Derbyshire will never be settled. My mother declares it possessed the worst qualities of both counties. I have repeated your defence to her, and she says it is precisely what one ought to expect of a gentleman from Derbyshire. You must not take this too much to heart, for she speaks very kindly of you upon every other subject.

I ought perhaps to repent of recommending a book which has robbed you of two nights’ sleep, yet your account of it makes repentance difficult. I like particularly your observation that its principal characters are improved by discovering themselves to have been mistaken. I wonder, though, whether either would have managed the discovery without the other. As for your promise to practise admitting your errors in my company until you are perfect, I must beg you not to succeed too quickly. It would deprive me of a very agreeable occupation.

I am prepared to hear your defence of Lady Catherine, but shall expect you to explain why an interest in another person’s shelves and closets requires so many questions and so few invitations. My aunt has heard of your argument, and assures me that her cupboards will bear inspection, although she thinks the principle a very dangerous one. She bids me say that she will be delighted to receive you on Friday the third of November at eleven o’clock, when my father and mother will also be at home. I hope the roads will be kind to you. If they are not, I trust you will postpone your journey rather than attempt it for the sake of proving a point about the superiority of Derbyshire weather.

You speak of the distance to Hampshire as though it had come as an unpleasant surprise. I am sorry it should be so far from your home, though it would be ungracious to deny that I am pleased thirty miles seem to you so readily travelled. We shall have much to discuss when you arrive: the conclusion of the novel, Sir Humphry Davy’s endeavours, and whether a man may be acquitted of pride while persisting in a defence he admits to be indefensible.

Pray accept my parents’ compliments, and believe me,

I remain, Sir,

Your obliged and sincere servant,

Virginia P. Torrence

P.S. My aunt has ordered the good tea to be brought out for Friday. I think this a very great mark of favour, for she did not offer it to the curate last week, and he stayed to dinner.
