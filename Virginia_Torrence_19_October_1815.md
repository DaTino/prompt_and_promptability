At Mrs. Ashcombe’s house, near Congleton, Cheshire

Thursday, 19th October 1815

Dear Mr. Ainesbrough,

Your letter gave us very great pleasure, though I must begin by correcting one part of it. The rain overtook us scarcely six miles from Buxton, and my mother, who had already decided that the Derbyshire air was determined upon her destruction, took it as a confirmation of all her fears. Happily, she is quite recovered from her cold. We are to remain with my aunt in Cheshire for some weeks before returning to Hampshire, and so you may still direct your letters here.

I am particularly gratified to learn that you have taken up Pride and Prejudice. I hope, however, that you will not discover too close a resemblance between yourself and Mr. Darcy. I had supposed you a little readier than he to admit that another person might be right, even when that person has the misfortune to be a young lady. As for Mr. Collins, I shall not defend him. My grandfather was a clergyman, and I feel it my duty to protest against such a representative of his profession. I believe Mr. Collins would profit more by listening to a sermon than by delivering one.

I remember very well what you told me of the miners, and shall be sincerely glad if Sir Humphry Davy can contrive a means of preserving them from so dreadful a danger. I understand little of the science, but enough of its purpose to think your interest in it entirely creditable. Pray do not tell your brother that this judgement is owing to the eloquence of the gentleman who first explained the matter to me. He would be quite mistaken, of course.

My father desires me to say that he would be happy to receive you at my aunt’s house, should your engagements bring you into this part of Cheshire before our departure. My mother promises a better fire than any we enjoyed at the Crescent. For myself, I should be glad to hear your final opinion of the novel, and to discover whether you still expect me to defend Mr. Collins when we meet. I fear you will find me disappointingly reasonable upon that subject.

Pray accept my father’s compliments and my mother’s good wishes.

I remain, Sir,

Your obliged and obedient servant,

Virginia P. Torrence

P.S. Since you confess to having begun your letter five times, I ought perhaps to own that I have rewritten this postscript twice. You must not think me quite so composed a correspondent as I appear.
